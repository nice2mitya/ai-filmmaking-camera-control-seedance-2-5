# Direct static clay previs like operators

A previs camera feels professional when it obeys four constraints real crews cannot escape: a real lens at a real distance, a rig with mass and a speed limit, a move with a reason that starts and ends on a composed frame, and cuts that respect the axis and the viewer's eye. The best-sourced numbers anchor that rulebook. Pans stay under one frame width per 7 seconds at 24 fps (about 5.6°/s on a 35 mm Super 35 lens, 2.4°/s on an 85 mm) ([RED](https://www.reddigitalcinema.com/red-101/camera-panning-speed)). Faces need the lens at least about 1.5 m away to look natural ([Ward et al., PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5876805/)). Cuts on the same subject change angle by at least 30° ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/30-degree_rule)). Viewers prefer to lose about 4 frames of action across a match cut rather than repeat it ([Hecht & Kalkofen 2009](https://experimental.psychologie.uni-mainz.de/files/2024/06/2009hecht-kalkofen.pdf)). Contemporary shots average about 4 s, action 2 to 3 s ([Cutting et al. 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3485803/)). Scenes go wide, then close, then wide again ([Cutting 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5133278/)). Because the characters here are static placeholders with no faces that act, the camera, the blocking of rigid figures, an environmental moving layer and the cut must carry all of the emotion that a performance would normally supply. That raises the stakes on every rule below. For AI video, every vendor guide converges on one camera move per shot, named explicitly with shot size, angle and lens, and labelled shots for multi-shot. The fastest route to the same camera in AI video is to render the Blender previs and feed it to the model as a camera reference. Throughout, **sourced facts carry a citation**, and numbers marked *(heuristic)* or *(calc)* are working defaults derived by the researchers to test, not published standards.

## Shot size is set by where the frame cuts the body, and the eyes ride the upper third

The one hard framing rule in the sources is **never cut at a joint**: not at the ankles for a full shot, the knees for a medium, or the wrists in a tighter shot, because the figure reads as amputated ([Nikon](https://www.nikonusa.com/en/learn-and-explore/a/tips-and-techniques/video-composition-rules-establishing-medium-and-close-up-shots.html); [LibreTexts](https://human.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Theater%2C_Film%2C_and_Storytelling/Video_Production_Handbook/08%3A_Video_Aesthetics/8.01%3A_Basic_Shot_Sizes)). Headroom is set by placing the eyes near the upper-third line ([ASU](https://www.asu.edu/alti/ltlab/tutorials/video/basics/comp1_hdrm.htm)). Too much headroom makes the subject sink and too little cramps it ([NFSA](https://www.nfsa.gov.au/sites/default/files/2023-03/eds_cam_framing.pdf)). Lead room goes in the direction a subject faces or moves ([Wikipedia: Lead room](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead_room)). For clay figures with no faces, the head's orientation is the only "look", so lead room and eyeline do the work that eyes normally do. Point the placeholder's head precisely and frame to it.

| Size | Bottom edge | Use (convention) | Previs lens, S35 *(heuristic)* |
|---|---|---|---|
| EWS | figure under ~1/10 of frame height | place, scale, isolation, act openings | 18–32 mm |
| WS / LS | full body plus space | geography, blocking | 24–35 mm |
| FS | just below feet, never at ankles | full body language, entrances | 27–35 mm |
| Cowboy / MWS | mid-thigh, above knee | stance, groups | 32–40 mm |
| MS | at or just below the waist | default coverage, hands visible | 35–50 mm |
| MCU | mid-chest | dialogue default | 40–65 mm |
| CU | top of shoulders, never at chin or neck | decision, peak emotion | 50–85 mm |
| ECU | detail; crops forehead and chin | the single peak of a sequence | 85–100 mm or macro |
| Insert | object fills frame | information the viewer must read | any; match the scene |

Angles read as status. Eye level is neutral, a high camera diminishes and a low camera empowers. A Dutch tilt of **15–25° reads as clearly noticeable** ([Adobe](https://www.adobe.com/creativecloud/video/production/cinematography/camera-shots-and-angles/dutch-angle-shot.html)), with 10–15° subtle and up to 45° extreme ([Skillshare](https://www.skillshare.com/en/blog/the-dutch-angle-a-cinematic-shot-for-dramatic-impact/)). No source gives degree values for high and low angles, so the following are *(heuristic)*. Put the lens within ±5 cm of eye height for neutral shots. Raise or lower it 15–30 cm with a 5–10° tilt for a status shift nobody notices. Go 0.5–1 m above with a 20–35° tilt for vulnerability or surveillance, and use chest or waist height with a 10–25° up-tilt for power. Using the same low angle on both sides of a conversation cancels the effect. In an over-the-shoulder shot the standard is a soft "dirty" shoulder sliver ([No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/over-the-shoulder-shot-examples-definition)), with the camera near shoulder height and without so much back-of-head that it competes with the far figure ([MasterClass](https://www.masterclass.com/articles/over-the-shoulder-shot)). Giving the shoulder about 1/4–1/3 of the frame is *(heuristic)*.

Composition defaults follow from those rules. Place the face on the third opposite its look, with looking room on the long side. Short-siding, where the figure looks toward the near edge, reads as confinement, guilt or panic ([Melies](https://melies.co/cinematic-techniques/composition/short-siding)). As a default it becomes a gimmick, so reserve it for a story turn ([Reel3](https://reel3.com/short-siding-how-david-fincher-and-nicolas-winding-refn-get-it-right/)). The 2.39:1 ratio favours landscapes and two-shots but cramps singles, so one filmmaker chose 1.85 for close-ups ([PremiumBeat](https://www.premiumbeat.com/blog/aspect-ratios-explained-when-to-use-the-major-three)). Choose the ratio for the whole film, not per shot ([In Depth Cine](https://www.indepthcine.com/videos/aspect-ratio)). Three depth planes give a flat screen depth *(heuristic)*: a soft foreground element 0.5–1 m from a 25–35 mm lens, the subject in the midground, and a readable background. For 9:16 delivery, centre-weighted, vertically stacked layers replace left and right thirds *(heuristic)*.

## Lens choice is really a camera-distance choice

Perspective comes from camera-to-subject distance. Focal length only decides how far away you must stand for a given size. The facial evidence is unusually solid: **at 30 cm, nose breadth reads about 30% larger, and at 1.5 m there is no perceived distortion** ([Ward et al. 2018, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5876805/)). That explains why practitioners cluster at **32–40 mm for normal coverage and 50–75 mm for close-ups**. Deakins, as summarised secondhand, uses 32–35 mm for standard shots, 35–40 mm for mids and 50–75 mm for CUs, and prefers moving the camera to zooming ([CineD](http://www.cined.com/what-lens-would-roger-deakins-choose-insights-for-cinema-shooters)). The wide-lens signatures are deliberate departures from that norm. Kubrick shot *The Shining* on an 18 mm lens that "worked for furniture but not faces" ([Filmmaker Magazine](https://filmmakermagazine.com/?p=94596)). Kaminski used ~29 mm close-ups on *Schindler's List* for realism ([In Depth Cine](https://www.indepthcine.com/videos/januszkaminski)). Kurosawa's telephotos compress action seen from a distance ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filmmaking_technique_of_Akira_Kurosawa)). The claim that Spielberg "sees in 21 mm" is folklore with no primary source. Clay placeholders do not suffer nose distortion in any meaningful way, but viewers still read the space a lens implies. A wide lens close in will feel grotesque or immersive even on clay.

The decision bands are *(heuristic)*. Use 14–24 mm to expand space and exaggerate speed toward the lens, for establishing interiors and unease; keep verticals level. Use 27–35 mm for masters and walk-and-talks where the world matters, 40–50 mm for neutral MS, MCU and OTS, and 65–85 mm for close-ups with a simplified, soft background. Use 100–200 mm for compression, surveillance and crowds: lateral motion reads fast and motion toward the lens reads slow. Reverse singles must **match focal length, camera distance and angle off the line** so head sizes match. A deliberately bigger head marks whom to watch ([Animation Mentor](https://newsletters.animationmentor.com/newsletter/0206/feature_film.html)).

Depth of field follows the same logic. Narrative work centres on about **T2.8**, roughly two stops down from a fast prime, for sharpness and pullable focus ([Wolfcrow](https://wolfcrow.com/what-is-the-best-aperture-for-filmmaking/), a blog). Messerschmidt shot *Mank* on Super 35 specifically to get more depth ([Musicbed](https://musicbed.com/articles/?p=4691)). The practical numbers *(calc)* are 85 mm at T2.8 focused at 1.5 m holding about **4 cm** sharp, 50 mm at T2.8 about 12 cm, 32 mm at T2.8 at 2 m about 55 cm, and 18 mm at T8 sharp from 0.9 m to infinity. As a share of frame width, the background blur disc is about 10% at 85 mm T2, 2.5% at 50 mm T2.8 and 1% at 32 mm T2.8, where the background stays readable. The rules are *(heuristic)*. Use deep focus (T4–T8, 35 mm or wider) when staging in depth carries meaning. Use shallow focus (T1.4–T2, 65 mm or longer) for isolation and intimacy, which also hides an unfinished clay set. A focus puller works from measured marks to each actor ([Wikipedia: Focus puller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Focus_puller)). In CG, keyframe focus distance to the near eye and rack only on a motivation: a turn, a line, a sound or an entrance. Start the rack on the beat, ease it, and take 0.5–1.5 s for a look, under 0.3 s for shock, and 2–4 s for a reveal *(heuristic; no source gives durations)*. Modulating FOV by 1–3% during a rack mimics focus breathing *(heuristic)*.

## Choose the move by its meaning, then obey the rig's physics

Movement meanings are stock and well catalogued ([StudioBinder](https://www.studiobinder.com/blog/different-types-of-camera-movements-in-film/)). Push-in means tension or access to an inner state. Pull-out means isolation or a reveal of context. Arc means energy or unease, and roll means instability. A crane gives scale, handheld gives a raw, subjective witness, and a static frame gives the blocking room to play. With static figures, the push-in and the held frame become the substitutes for acting. Dean Cundey describes Spielberg's camera moving **closer as danger approaches** ([StudioBinder](https://www.studiobinder.com/blog/jurassic-park-cinematography/)), and that is the single most transferable rule for clay. The motivation rules are *(heuristic, consistent with the sources)*. When the subject moves, move with it, starting a few frames after it starts and settling after it stops. For a reveal, end on a frame composed around the new information. For a look, pan after the head turns. For a realisation, push in; for defeat, pull out. With no reason, lock off. Every move begins and ends on a composed frame held for 0.5–1 s.

The zoom versus dolly decision is optical, not stylistic. **A dolly changes perspective and parallax; a zoom scales the whole frame uniformly** ([Morphic](https://morphic.com/ai-glossary/zoom)). If the background changes size relative to the subject, it was a dolly. Use a dolly or push-in when the viewer should physically approach. Use a zoom only for a found or observational camera that cannot move, a crash zoom for comic or shock emphasis, or a slow creep zoom for paranoia *(craft convention)*. The dolly zoom keeps the subject width W constant through **distance = W / (2·tan(FOV/2))**, equivalent to d₂/d₁ = f₂/f₁ ([Wikipedia: Dolly zoom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dolly_zoom); [UPenn CIS581](https://alliance.seas.upenn.edu/~cis581/Lectures/Fall2018/cis581-2018-projection.pdf)). Dolly in while zooming out to push the background away (*Jaws*); dolly out while zooming in to make it loom (*Fellowship*). The speeds must match exactly or the effect fails ([StudioBinder](https://www.studiobinder.com/blog/best-dolly-zoom-vertigo-effect/)). In CG, animate focal length with an ease and drive distance from it with a driver or expression, never with two independent key sets. A 3–4× focal ratio (24→70–100 mm) gives a strong stretch, and under 1.5× is subtle *(heuristic)*.

Speeds have one hard published anchor. RED's rule is to pan **no faster than one full image width every 7 seconds** at 24 fps and 180° shutter, as a starting point. Whip pans and fast pans tracking a close subject are exempt ([RED](https://www.reddigitalcinema.com/red-101/camera-panning-speed)). That gives a maximum of hFOV/7 per second (the column above), and the same limit applies to tilts using the vertical FOV. Everything else is *(heuristic)* because no published dolly or crane working-speed table was found. Imperceptible creeps run at 0.05–0.15 m/s, emotional pushes at 0.15–0.4 m/s over 4–10 s, a punch-in at 0.5–1.5 m in 1–2 s, and a walking follow at 1.0–1.5 m/s. A crane boom moves at 0.3–1.0 m/s over 4–8 s. The pacing language matches: a slow dolly "over many seconds" builds tension, and one over "a second or two" is an exclamation point ([Morphic](https://morphic.com/ai-glossary/Dolly-In)). Hard ceilings exist. Technocrane telescopes top out around **0.75–2.2 m/s** ([Wikipedia: Technocrane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technocrane); [Filmotechnic](https://filmotechnic.nl/technoscope)). Drone reveals run at about 1.8–2.7 m/s and fly-overs at 3.6–6.7 m/s, flown "more slowly than feels natural" ([PremiumBeat](https://www.premiumbeat.com/blog/8-flight-tips-drone-footage)). Cinematic cinewhoops fly at about 32 km/h, a manufacturer figure ([RouteNote](https://licensing.routenote.com/blog/what-is-a-cinewhoop-drone/)). Render with motion blur at a 180° shutter (Blender shutter 0.5) or fast moves strobe even within the rule.

Real rigs have mass, and that is what CG cameras lack. Steadicam balance is defined by a **2–3 s drop time, with about 2.5 s typical**. Shorter drops pendulum more on starts and stops ([DVInfo](https://www.dvinfo.net/forum/archive/index.php/t-30014.html)). Handheld shake is **rotation-dominant and layered: about 0.1–0.5 Hz, 0.8–1.5 Hz and 3–4 Hz**. Wide lenses take larger amplitude and telephotos smaller, and noise is applied after the move is computed ([Unity Cinemachine](https://docs.unity3d.com/Packages/com.unity.cinemachine@3.1/manual/CinemachineNoiseProfiles.html)). Standing operators shake most at 3–5 Hz and vehicles at 6–10 Hz ([USPTO 8970712](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/8970712)). A critically damped spring (damping = 2·√stiffness) converges without overshoot ([Unreal FCriticalDamper](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/unreal-engine/API/Plugins/GameplayCameras/FCriticalDamper)). Recorded human shake beats synthetic noise, which is why Ian Hubert's tracked CC0 shakes in Camera Shakify are the default choice in Blender ([GitHub](https://github.com/EatTheFuture/camera_shakify)). The Third Floor gets physical feel by having directors and DPs operate tracked virtual cameras in real time ([CGW](https://www.cgw.com/Press-Center/Web-Exclusives/2019/Third-Floor-At-the-Forefront-of-Real-Time-Vis.aspx)). The per-rig spring profiles below are *(heuristic starting values)* derived from those sources.

|---|---|---|---|
| Tripod / fluid head | never a snap start | critical; settle 0.15–0.3 s | none |
| Dolly on track | 0.5–1.5 s | ratio 0.8–0.9; 1–3% overshoot, settle ~0.5 s | track buzz under 0.02° |
| Crane / jib | 1–2 s | ratio 0.7–0.85; float at top of boom; head lags arm | none |

| Handheld | operator reframes 4–8 frames late, slight overshoot | n/a | 0.2–0.3 Hz at 0.3–1°; ~1 Hz at 0.2–0.5°; 3–5 Hz at 0.05–0.15°; halve on telephoto |
| Drone | 1–3 s | very smooth | yaw/altitude wander ~0.1 Hz; gust impulses |
| FPV | continuous | n/a | banks 10–30° into turns; propwash buzz |

The keyframe rules are *(heuristic)*. Use a trapezoid velocity profile with eases of 15–30% of the move each; moves under about 1.5 s can be a pure S-curve. When following action, make the ease-out no longer than the ease-in; for a reveal, make it longer so the camera lands gently. Keep one primary axis, with any secondary axis at 10–30% of it. Drive rotation from a lagging look-at target rather than hand-keyed Euler angles. Tag every camera with its rig and reject paths outside that rig's envelope. Techvis practice confirms the principle: when a previs move is physically impossible, adjust the ratio of subject to camera motion, and an orbit needs a flawless ellipse to read as plausible ([AWN: Casey Schatz](https://awn.com/vfxworld/casey-schatz-talks-techvis-martian)). The common fakes are linear speed (robotic), long eases on short moves (floaty), identical shake on every lens, a zoom where a dolly was meant, and three or more axes changing at once.

## Cut on the axis, at least 30° apart, and lose a few frames of action

Continuity rests on three sourced rules. **Keep every camera on one side of the action axis.** Cross the line only through a neutral on-axis shot, an on-screen camera move across it, or a cutaway ([StudioBinder](https://www.studiobinder.com/blog/what-is-the-180-degree-rule/)). Successive shots of the same subject **move at least 30°** around it ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/30-degree_rule)), and too large a change disorients ([RouteNote](https://licensing.routenote.com/blog/what-is-the-30-degree-rule/)). Screen direction must persist across cuts or a subject reads as turning back. In a chase, switch the side you shoot from and the cars "will no longer be chasing each other" ([Videomaker](https://www.videomaker.com/article/12986-screen-direction)). No source quantifies an axial size change, so treat this as *(heuristic)*: on the same axis, jump at least two named sizes (MS to CU), or change the angle 30–60° and the size together. In three.js or Blender, compute the line between the two figures currently engaged each beat, and reject any camera on the wrong side by the sign of the cross product unless the shot is flagged as a deliberate crossing.

Matching action has a surprising empirical answer. Editors' lore says to overlap 2–5 frames, but observers adjusting animated match cuts **preferred a gap averaging ~182 ms, about 4 frames at 24 fps**. On cuts from close to wide the preferred gaps ran 154–623 ms, while cuts from wide to close preferred little gap or a slight overlap ([Hecht & Kalkofen 2009](https://experimental.psychologie.uni-mainz.de/files/2024/06/2009hecht-kalkofen.pdf)). That experiment used computer animation, so it applies directly here. Drop 4–8 frames on CU-to-WS cuts and use a straight match or a 0–2 frame overlap on WS-to-CU. In AI multi-shot, start shot B slightly later in the action than shot A ended. Sound bridges of about **0.5 s** are the dialogue default for J- and L-cuts ([Filmdaft](https://filmdaft.com/the-l-cut-and-j-cut-how-film-editors-use-audio-to-control-time/)). Cuts make up **almost 99%** of modern transitions; dissolves are rare ([Cutting et al. 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3485803/)). About 90% of shots contain a face, and the rest are mostly inserts, POVs or establishing shots ([Cutting 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5133278/)). With faceless figures, inserts, POVs and the placeholder's head orientation must carry the attention a face would.

When to cut follows Murch's ranking: **emotion 51%, story 23%, rhythm 10%, eye-trace 7%, 2D plane 5%, 3D space 4%**. A higher criterion outweighs everything below it, and the percentages are illustrative ([UT Austin RTF](https://cloud.wikis.utexas.edu/wiki/spaces/rtf318/pages/80022510/Murch+s+Rule+of+Six)). Murch's cut-on-the-blink is moot for clay, but its logic survives: cut where a thought ends. On *Fury Road*, Miller demanded crosshairs on the point of interest so editor Margaret Sixel could cut very fast and stay readable, across about 2,700 shots ([Eugene Wei](https://www.eugenewei.com/blog/2015/5/31/creating-order-out-of-the-chaos-of-mad-max-fury-road)). Shorter shots carry more motion (r = −.46) ([Cutting et al. 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3485803/)), and close-ups run shorter than wides because they read faster ([Cornell Chronicle](https://news.cornell.edu/node/285439)). The working rules are *(heuristic)*. Keep the point of interest within about 10–15% of frame width of where the eye already is. Crosshair-frame every shot under 1.5 s. When a figure turns its head off-screen, cut to what it sees, then back.

Pacing data is robust. Hollywood ASL fell from **about 10 s in the 1930s–40s to under 4 s after 2000** ([Cutting et al. 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3485803/)). Bordwell's action cases run *Bourne Supremacy* and *Batman Begins* at 1.9 s ([Bordwell](https://davidbordwell.net/blog/category/hollywood-aesthetic-traditions/page/56)). Crowd-sourced Cinemetrics averages put action at 4.0 s and horror at 15.7 s ([Stephen Follows](https://stephenfollows.com/many-shots-average-movie/)). The gap between those figures reflects sampling, and Barry Salt warns that equal ASLs can hide different shot mixes. Within films, **shot durations "fall precipitously during the first part of the climax" and "rise smartly during the epilog"**. Establishing shots run longer, and scenes open wide, move in to faces and widen before the next scene, with shot scale the strongest scene-change cue ([Cutting 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5133278/)). A practitioner floor is about 3 s of usable footage per shot ([MarketScale](https://www.marketscale.com/industries/pro-av/cinematography-tutorial-following-the-three-second-rule.md)), yet *Transformers 2* contains 0.2 s shots ([Stephen Follows](https://stephenfollows.com/many-shots-average-movie/)).

## Story beats map to camera choices, and action lives on clarity

The beat-to-camera mappings with real sources are three. **Suspense comes from telling the audience first.** Hitchcock's bomb under the table turns an ordinary conversation into dread once viewers know, and surprise should be saved for the twist ([No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/alfred-hitchcock-and-francois-truffaut-explain-surprise-vs-suspense)). **Wonder comes from the Spielberg face**, a reaction close-up held before the object is shown ([Kottke on Kevin B. Lee](https://kottke.org/16/10/the-spielberg-face)). **Danger draws the camera closer** (Cundey). For faceless figures, the Spielberg face becomes a push and a hold rather than an expression. The figure notices in a medium shot, the camera dollies (not zooms) to a close shot over 3–6 s while the head is turned off-axis near the lens, holds, then cuts to the object, wide and often low *(heuristic recipe)*. The Kuleshov principle that a neutral face takes its meaning from the adjacent shot is what makes this work on clay *(craft convention)*. The other mappings are *(heuristic)*. For suspense, show the threat in a clear insert, play long held shots, and return to the threat one size tighter each time. Withhold surprise and reveal it on a cut or fast move, once per sequence. Stage discovery as search (MS), find (POV or insert), react. Give power the lower camera, the larger frame share and the stable frame, and shift power through blocking, such as one figure rising, rather than a cut. Show isolation with a small figure, large negative space or a pull-out. After a peak, release with the widest, stillest, longest-held shot of the sequence. A reveal by move keeps cause and space continuous: the *Jurassic Park* lab scene opens with a 44 s backward move that keeps exposing new foreground ([Senses of Cinema](https://www.sensesofcinema.com/2003/steven-spielberg/spielberg_symposium_films_and_moments/)). A reveal by cut is a punch.

Director methods translate into placeholder-friendly rules. **Spielberg's oners run about 1–2 minutes** and change shot size by moving actors and camera, so the take never feels kinetic ([No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/2014/05/study-steven-spielbergs-cinematography-help-you-master-long-take)). Because the figures are static, that means animating rigid figures between marks while keeping the camera move small, with each stop a composition an editor would have cut to. Kurosawa layers weather, groups, individuals, camera and the cut, and resets rhythm by ending on stillness and cutting into motion ([Open Culture](https://openculture.com/category/film/page/153)). For clay this is the most valuable idea in the notes: **always give the frame one moving layer** (wind, rain, smoke, dust, banners), because nothing else in it acts *(heuristic)*. Cuarón keeps long takes wide so character and environment carry equal weight ([DGA Quarterly](https://www.dga.org/craft/dgaq/issues/1303-summer-2013/alfonso-cuaron)), and *Children of Men* hid digital stitches inside its takes ([Teknokultura](https://revistas.ucm.es/index.php/TEKN/article/download/104334/4564456574719/4564456778303)). That is how to join AI clips: behind whip pans, foreground wipes or darkness. Edgar Wright gets comedy from what is in or out of frame, from entrances and exits, and from "there and back again" ([Laughing Squid](https://laughingsquid.com/how-to-do-visual-comedy-an-analysis-of-director-edgar-wrights-humorous-cinematic-styles-and-techniques/)). Those gags need only blocking, not faces.

Dialogue coverage follows the triangle. Put every camera on one side of the line, the master at the apex, three-quarter singles at the base corners with lead room, and OTS shots sliding along the base. Any position inside the triangle cuts with any other ([Shoot First](https://www.shootfirst.art/the-triangle-system)). Modern practice gives "nearly every line or reaction" its own shot, and the sustained two-shot is "practically an endangered species". Lubitsch instead saved his closest shots for the most intense exchange ([Bordwell](https://davidbordwell.net/blog/2007/05/27/intensified-continuity-revisited/)). For previs, the Lubitsch rule is the better default *(heuristic)*. Step in on both sides together, master (27–35 mm) to OTS pair (40–50 mm) to clean singles (50–75 mm) to a CU (75–85 mm) at the turn, then cut back to a two-shot to reset geography. Use an ECU at most once. Tightening on one side only means that character has taken the scene. With three figures, treat the scene as two triangles sharing the centre figure, and cut on the head turn when the line moves. For a walk-and-talk, lead with a backward dolly or Steadicam on a 35–50 mm lens in a medium-wide two-shot, and keep the walkers' left-right order constant.

Action grammar starts with legibility. **Bordwell's first task is that viewers can tell who hits whom and which car chases which** ([Bordwell](https://davidbordwell.net/blog/category/national-cinemas-hong-kong/page/14)). His frame counts from a Jackie Chan fight show distant shots held longer than close ones: 26 and 16 frames for one throw, 28 frames for a hit with only 7 frames before impact. The rhythm is pause, burst, pause, with no cut before a blow completes. A repeated big impact plays as an axial cut-in, with the second shot exactly twice the first's 17 frames ([Bordwell, "Bond vs. Chan"](https://www.davidbordwell.net/blog/2010/09/15/bond-vs-chan-jackie-shows-how-its-done/)). Chan's principles include action and reaction in the same frame and "two good hits = one great hit" ([No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/2014/12/jackie-chans-9-principles-action-comedy)). Chases run on legible distance. Pass the pursued and then the pursuer by the same landmark a few seconds apart, and keep to about two vehicles, one obstacle and one deadline ([Cinapse](https://cinapse.co/?p=6810)). *Fury Road* flips a threat's screen direction with an eyeline and a pan over three shots ([Videomaker](https://www.videomaker.com/article/c3/7400-edit-suite-the-mysteries-of-screen-direction/)). *Top Gun: Maverick* covered cockpits with six cameras on 10–15 mm and 21–50 mm primes facing the pilot, the back-seater and forward over each shoulder, and planned the sun so the jets are backlit ([ASC](https://theasc.com/articles/top-gun-maverick)). The previs rules that follow are *(heuristic)*. Open with a 2–4 s geography wide and return to it every 4–6 shots or after any direction change. Store screen direction as a vector per sequence. Exaggerate speed with a low camera, a wide lens close in and foreground passing the lens, and use a long lens for "they're gaining". Finally, cut any shot that does not answer who is where, which way they are moving, or what changed. Fragmented, incoherent cutting is what Matthias Stork named "chaos cinema" ([Stork](https://scalar.usc.edu/works/film-studies-in-motion/chaos-cinema-part-1-by-matthias-stork)).

## Blender and three.js need real sensors, real primes and marker cuts

In Blender, set **Sensor Fit to Horizontal** so width is fixed and height derives from the aspect ratio ([Blender manual](https://projects.blender.org/blender/blender-manual/raw/branch/main/manual/render/cameras.rst)). Use 24.89 mm for Super 35 or 36 mm for full frame *(practice; the S35 width was not verified against an ARRI page)*. With Auto, a 9:16 render fits the sensor to the height and every lens becomes wider than intended. Use lens Shift, not rotation, to keep verticals straight. Drive depth of field from a Focus Object, since the F-Stop field controls blur only, and Cycles and EEVEE share one value ([Blender commit](https://developer.blender.org/rBbc3139d79226896cf6c2b0813a1188eda70fad09)). Turn on passepartout, safe areas and the Thirds guide. Restrict lenses to a real prime set (18, 25, 32, 40, 50, 75, 100, 135 mm), name cameras `sh010_cam`, and burn lens and camera metadata into every frame *(practice)*. Professional techvis delivers plan-view diagrams with camera, lens, frustum, path, dimensions and speeds ([VFX Voice](https://www.vfxvoice.com/a-visioneers-guide-to-the-galaxy/)), which is the standard to emulate. For rigs, a Pan empty (Z only) parents a Tilt empty (local X only), which parents the camera with roll on the camera itself *(practice)*. Set F-curve handles to Auto Clamped, "limited to prevent overshoots" ([Blender manual](https://projects.blender.org/blender/blender-manual/raw/branch/main/manual/editors/graph_editor/fcurves/properties.rst)). Layer Camera Shakify on a separate parent; version 0.5.1 fixes a Blender 5.0 driver break ([extensions.blender.org](https://extensions.blender.org/add-ons/camera-shakify)). For edits, Ctrl+B in the Timeline binds the selected camera to a marker, and moving the marker moves the cut ([Blender manual](https://docs.blender.org/manual/pt/4.2/animation/markers.html)). Work at 1 unit = 1 m, with standing eye level 1.5–1.7 m and seated 1.1–1.2 m, 24 fps, and shutter 0.5 *(practice)*.

In three.js, **`fov` is vertical and `filmGauge` defaults to 35 mm** on the larger axis. `setFocalLength(mm)` derives fov from the gauge, and `updateProjectionMatrix()` must follow any change ([three.js docs](https://threejs.org/docs/pages/PerspectiveCamera.html)). Set `filmGauge` to the same 24.89 or 36 used in Blender so a "50 mm" means the same view in both. A 16:9 S35 frame then gives vertical fov 22.6° at 35 mm and 15.9° at 50 mm *(calc)*. In Remotion, derive camera state from the frame number (spring, ease, noise), never from wall-clock time, so the springs stay deterministic and frame-rate independent, which the critical-damper sources stress ([GameDev.net](https://gamedev.net/forums/topic/710443-jitter-in-a-soft-follow-cam?page=1)).

## AI video prompts need one named move per shot, ending on a named frame

Every vendor guide converges on the same grammar, with model-specific syntax.

| Model | Sourced guidance |
|---|---|
| Seedance 2.x | Label "Shot 1: … Shot 2: …". Camera move first in each shot, then action, space, audio. Don't fix per-shot durations; output is ~5–15 s, 30 s on 2.5. Avoid stacking push + tilt + pan, avoid fast violent motion, put constraints at the end ([Runway guide](https://runway.com/resources/seedance-2-0-prompt-guide)). 60–100 words; "one continuous take, no cuts" for oners; assign reference roles, e.g. "@video1 = camera movement only", or lighting and framing leak ([heyuan110](https://www.heyuan110.com/posts/ai/2026-07-11-seedance-2-prompt-guide/)). |
| Kling 3.0 / Omni | Subject + move + purpose ("Camera tilts up from the shoes to the athlete's face"). Stability cue: "slow, stable pan left … no sudden motion". Omni storyboard sets size, perspective and camera per shot. Fails on stacked or contradictory moves and pull-backs without a start subject ([Kling blog](https://kling.ai/blog/kling-ai-camera-control-video-guide)). | Sora 2 | One camera setup, one action, one light recipe per shot. Shorter clips follow better, so two 4 s clips can beat one 8 s. Write "Anamorphic 2.0x lens, shallow DOF", not "cinematic". | Hailuo / MiniMax | Brackets placed inline at the action: "[Push in]", "[Truck left, Pan right]" (comma = simultaneous, separate brackets = sequence). One move best, two plenty; don't mix bracket and prose grammar ([Hailuo cheat sheet](https://hailuoai.video/pages/blog/camera-movement-prompt-cheatsheet)). Newer MiniMax-H3 may not document brackets ([minimax-ai.chat, unofficial](https://minimax-ai.chat/?p=2010)). |

The cross-model template *(heuristic, consistent with all guides)* runs: **[size], [angle and height], [lens mm, DOF], [one move + speed word + start→end framing]**, then the subject with two or three fixed traits, then one action as beats, then environment, light and style, then a positively phrased constraint line. An example is "Medium shot, eye level, 35 mm, deep focus. Slow dolly push over 6 seconds ending in a close-up, eases in and out. A matte grey clay figure in a long coat stands at a window facing frame-left; dust drifts through a side light. Locked horizon, camera on the left side of the action line." Say "dolly in" for travel and "zoom" only for a real zoom, since Veo and Hailuo define them differently. Say "static, locked-off tripod" when no move is wanted, because Seedance defaults to subtle drift without direction ([fliki](https://fliki.ai/blog/seedance-2-prompting-guide)). The editing rules carry into prompts. Use 2–4 shots per 10–15 s clip. Graphic or match cuts are the most reliable way to join independently generated clips: end shot A and start shot B with the same silhouette in the same frame position. Restate the axis side, screen direction and centred subject in every prompt of a sequence. The strongest bridge between the two halves of this pipeline is to **render the Blender clay previs and pass it as Seedance's @video camera reference, or its frames as first and last frames**. The camera then carries over exactly, and the prompt handles only look *(inference; no source measured it)*.

## Conclusion

The deepest implication is that static placeholders invert the usual hierarchy of a film frame. When nothing on screen can act, Murch's 51% for emotion has to be bought entirely through distance (push-ins as danger nears), time (holds longer than comfortable, then pause-burst-pause), the head orientation of a rigid figure, and one moving environmental layer. That makes the Spielberg, Kurosawa and Lubitsch habits more useful here than coverage-heavy intensified continuity, which assumes a fresh face for every line. Previs built this way also yields a reusable camera language for AI generation rather than a throwaway animatic, because the rig, lens and path survive into Seedance or Veo as reference.

The evidence has a clear shape. Optics, the pan-speed rule, continuity rules, ASL trends and the match-cut gap are solid. Nearly every speed for dolly, crane and rack, every spring constant, and every angle in degrees is researcher inference that nobody has published. The right move is to treat those values as parameters: tag cameras by rig, render a test of each profile, and keep the values that read as human operation. Per-model reliability for dolly zoom, rack focus and FPV in AI video is unmeasured, so test those first before building sequences on them.
